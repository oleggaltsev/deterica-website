Genesis · Prosthetic hand control · No electrodes

The hand follows
your own movement

The user moves the residual limb against the socket. A position sensor reads the movement. The hand follows the range the user calibrated. Two buttons select the grip. There is no electrode, no muscle signal, and no training on a signal that drifts with sweat or with a socket that shifts.

The problem

EMG control is a load the user carries all day

A myoelectric hand reads muscle signals through electrodes in the socket. The signal works in a fitting room. It is harder to keep in a life.

01 · Switching

Slow, and mentally tiring

"Too slow, unreliable, unintuitive and mentally exhausting."

That is how users of multi-function hands described grip switching by muscle contraction in a 2019 study. Most of them used only two or three functions, because more was confusing and hard to remember.

02 · The signal

It moves with the body

Temperature, humidity, sweat, socket displacement, and the weight of the arm all change the signal. The result is a signal artefact, and an unintended movement of the hand.

03 · The training

Long, and tiring

A user must learn to produce a contraction the electrodes can read, then keep it repeatable. A user who cannot produce a stable signal has no path into a multi-grip hand at all.

What this project does not claim

The literature does not show that people abandon electric devices more often than body-powered ones. For adults the rates are close: about 23 % for electric devices against about 26 % for body-powered ones. Comfort, socket fit, and weight lead the list of reasons, and price sits near the bottom.

So the claim here is narrow and it is the one the evidence supports: EMG control is a documented problem of cognitive load and usability. This control method removes that load. It does not, on its own, solve comfort, weight, or socket fit.

Why this exists

Genesis did not begin as a business plan. It began with my father

I went looking for a hand for him. What I found was a market where a good hand costs more than a car, takes months to obtain, and then asks the user to learn a muscle signal before it will move at all. For a lot of people that is not a choice between devices. It is the difference between a hand and no hand.

So I set out to build the thing I could not buy: control that works from a movement a person already has, and that the person can set up alone, at the kitchen table.

Somewhere along the way it stopped being only about him. The same wall stands in front of everybody who has been told that a multi-grip hand is either unaffordable or too hard to use. I would like to take that wall down for them too.

I do not build it alone. Siarhei Arefyev turned the control into working hardware on the first hand, and he is working with me on this project.

Oleg Haltsev · Founder, DETERICA LLC

The control

Movement in, position out

The residual limb already moves. The method uses that movement directly, as a position, and not as a signal that must be classified.

  • →
    The user turns the residual limb inside the socket.
  • →
    A position sensor measures the angle.
  • →
    The calibration maps that angle onto the user's own range, from fully open to fully closed.
  • →
    The hand holds the position the user gives it. Halfway is halfway.
  • →
    Two buttons step through the grips. The change applies at once, and the fingers ease into the new shape.

The user sets it up alone

The calibration is two button holds on the hand. It needs no clinic visit, no laptop, and no software that only a specialist can run. A user who changes their mind holds the button again.

It is the user's colour

The shell, the finger pads, and the buttons are each specified on their own. The hand can be matched to a skin tone, or made to stand out on purpose.

It charges on the arm

The hand runs on its own battery. An ordinary USB power bank tops that battery up, and the user keeps the hand on while it charges.

Who the method fits

It needs a preserved rotation of the residual limb. That is the eligibility criterion, and it is the first question to put to a prosthetist.

  1. STEP 1

    Save the open position

    The user holds one button for 2 seconds at the position that means "fully open".

  2. STEP 2

    Save the closed position

    The user holds the other button for 2 seconds at the position that means "fully closed".

  3. STEP 3

    Move

    The two saved points become the whole range. The kit clamps anything outside them.

  4. STEP 4

    Select a grip

    A short press steps forward or back through five grips. Nothing waits for a gate.

The kit

A lever, two buttons, one light

The lever stands in for the residual limb. The kit reads the lever, applies the same calibration, and runs the same control logic a hand would run. It works with no phone: the buttons, the light, and the calibration all work alone.

  • ✓
    USB-C powered. Version 1 has no battery
  • ✓
    Bluetooth Low Energy to the phone
  • ✓
    Calibration is stored on the kit and applied at power on
  • ✓
    It does not slide or tip when the user turns the lever

The app

The hand, on your phone

The app shows a virtual hand that follows the lever with no visible delay. It runs with no kit at all: a lever and two buttons on the screen drive the same control logic, so anybody can try the model before a kit exists.

  • ✓
    Five grips: power grip, pinch, point, rock, hook
  • ✓
    Left hand or right hand
  • ✓
    Calibrate from the app or from the kit buttons
  • ✓
    No account, and no server. Settings stay on the phone

The app

One control logic. Two places it can run

The kit and the app run the same control logic, and a recorded trace holds them to it: the app replays every tick the kit recorded and compares every output field. A behaviour that drifts in one place turns a test red.

  • ✓
    The kit is the authority. When a kit is connected, the app runs no control tick of its own. Two copies would fight for the screen
  • ✓
    The app works with no kit. The screen gives a lever and two buttons, and nothing more than a kit has
  • ✓
    It never lies about a command. The app does not report success until the kit acknowledges it
  • ✓
    The lever is not smoothed. The finger the lever drives answers on the tick the lever moves

FAQ

Questions, answered plainly

Is this a medical device?

No. The Developer Kit is a demonstration kit and a training kit. It is not a medical device, and nothing on this site or in the app claims a therapeutic, rehabilitative, or diagnostic benefit.

Can I buy a prosthetic hand from you today?

No. The hand is not for sale yet. What is ready now is the Developer Kit: the control, a desktop kit, and the app. If you want to follow the hand, write to us and we will keep you informed.

Who can use this control method?

It needs a preserved rotation of the residual limb, because that rotation is the input. A prosthetist is the right person to say whether a particular limb has it.

Why is the app useful with no kit?

Because the control model is the thing worth trying, and the app runs the same logic. A lever and two buttons on the screen behave as the kit behaves, so you can judge proportional control and grip selection before you own hardware.

Is the control logic open?

Not at present. The control library, the firmware, and the app are private. If you have a research or a teaching use for them, write to us and describe it.
